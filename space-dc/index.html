LEO 550 km · 53°

Cheapest to reach and closest to users. Earth blocks the Sun for up to ~36 of every ~96 minutes, so the whole machine goes through a hot/cold cycle about 15 times a day. Every eclipse needs batteries (heavy) or throttled compute (chips cycle hot and cold).

Dawn–dusk sun-synchronous, 650 km

The orbit Google chose for Project Suncatcher. The plane stays over the day/night line, so the panels almost never see Earth's shadow and power is nearly continuous. The catch: the full heat load never stops, one side is always in sunlight and the other always in shade, and for roughly three months around one solstice it still dips into shadow for up to ~20 minutes per orbit.

GEO

Sunlit about 99% of the year, with eclipses only for ~6 weeks around each equinox (up to ~70 min once a day). Earth is tiny from here, so radiators see an almost perfectly cold sky. The costs: ~240 ms round-trip latency, harsh electron radiation, and far fewer tonnes per launch.

The radiator is the data center's cooling tower

In vacuum there is no air or water to carry heat away. A radiator rejects q = ε σ T⁴ per square metre per face. At room temperature that is only a few hundred watts per m², so a megawatt needs a radiator the size of several tennis courts.

The heat path

  • GPU junction ~85 °C → cold plate → pumped coolant loop (the ISS uses ammonia) → radiator panels at the setpoint.
  • Radiators are hung edge-on to the Sun and in the shadow of the solar array. Here the radiator is a keel behind the array, whose plane also points its edge at Earth.
  • In LEO, Earth still fills about a third of the sky. It shines ~237 W/m² of infrared and reflects ~30% of sunlight onto the panels. In GEO both nearly vanish.

Scale check

The ISS rejects about 70 kW through its external ammonia loops. One 1 MW module needs roughly 15× that, and a 1 GW campus needs roughly 15,000× that: square kilometres of radiator.

What can go wrong

  • Micrometeoroids puncture tubes, so loops must be split into isolated sections or use heat pipes.
  • Freezing. Water freezes at 0 °C and ammonia at −78 °C. When compute throttles or a bypass valve closes off panels, idle panels cool toward Earth's IR temperature in LEO, and much colder in GEO.
  • The hot back of the solar array and neighbouring satellites' radiators take up part of the radiator's view of cold space.
  • Pumps, valves and seals must run for 5+ years with nobody to service them.

Why cycling matters

  • LEO: ~5,000 cycles a year. A 5-year mission is ~25,000 cycles for bolted joints, bonded inserts, composite laminates (microcracking) and solar-cell interconnects.
  • Chips. Silicon (2.6 ppm/K) is soldered to organic substrates (~16 ppm/K). Solder-joint life scales roughly with ΔT⁻² (Coffin–Manson), so halving the swing buys ~4× life. Throttling in eclipse makes the GPUs cycle too. Batteries keep them steady.
  • Thermal snap. Hubble's first solar arrays flexed every time it crossed into or out of shadow, shaking the telescope. They were replaced in 1993. Laser links to a neighbour 200 m away need microradian pointing, so a structure that jumps at every terminator crossing is a problem.
  • Dawn–dusk SSO removes most of the cycles but leaves a permanent gradient: sun side ~+60 °C, shade side far colder. That bends the structure all the time. The bend is steady and predictable, but it has to be designed in.

How the Sun drives everything

  • Above the atmosphere sunlight is 1,361 W/m². Triple-junction cells convert ~30% at 28 °C. About 70% of the light that hits the array becomes heat in the array.
  • Hot cells are less efficient: about −0.2 %/K for triple-junction and −0.45 %/K for silicon. A LEO array at +60 °C loses ~7% of its rated output.
  • In LEO the back of the array also takes Earth albedo, up to ~400 W/m² at local noon.
  • In shadow the array falls to −70…−90 °C in LEO and below −150 °C in a long GEO eclipse. On leaving shadow, the cold cells give ~25–30% higher voltage for a few minutes, and the power electronics must tolerate it.
  • Radiation and UV slowly darken the cells (~1–3 %/yr in LEO). The model uses a 90% end-of-life factor.
  • Google estimates a panel in dawn–dusk SSO makes up to ~8× more energy per year than the same panel at a mid-latitude site on the ground.

How many data centers can fly together?

Training large models needs tens of terabits per second between accelerators. Received laser power falls with distance squared, so the modules must fly close together. Google's Suncatcher study describes 81 satellites within about 1 km radius, 100–200 m apart, and a bench link reaching 1.6 Tbps with one transceiver pair.

Challenges that grow with the cluster

  • Formation keeping. Earth's oblateness (J2) and drag pull neighbours apart. Close formations need constant small burns and reliable collision avoidance, even when a satellite fails.
  • Shading. The lattice must face the Sun so no module shades another's array. The one-degree-of-freedom answer: arrange the lattice in the plane perpendicular to the Sun.
  • Radiators see each other. Facing neighbours are warm, not 3 K space. Interior modules lose some rejection and run warmer, which you can see in Thermal IR view.
  • Debris. One fragmentation inside a 1 km cluster reaches everyone. SSO is already the most crowded band.

What is solved, what is not

Shown or solved
  • Power. Unfiltered sunlight, no weather. Dawn–dusk SSO gives near-continuous generation.
  • Radiative cooling. Well understood. Pumped ammonia loops have run on the ISS for 25 years (~70 kW).
  • GPUs in orbit. Starcloud-1 flew an NVIDIA H100 in LEO in Nov 2025.
  • Radiation. Google reports its Trillium TPU took 15 krad(Si) of protons with no hard failures. HBM showed errors from ~2 krad, against ~0.75 krad expected over 5 shielded years.
  • Optical links. 1.6 Tbps on the bench. Laser crosslinks already run in the Starlink constellation.
Still open
  • Radiator scale. ~1,000–2,000 m² per MW, so km² per GW, deployed flat, leak-free and pointed edge-on.
  • Mass and launch. Tens of tonnes per MW. Google's analysis needs launch below ~$200/kg (mid-2030s) to match ground power costs.
  • Fatigue. ~5,000 cycles a year in LEO. Permanent gradients in SSO.
  • MW-class two-phase loops in microgravity, pump life, micrometeoroid leaks, freeze protection.
  • Servicing. No technicians. Failed GPUs stay failed.
  • Tight formations. Collision risk, debris, crowded SSO.
  • Eclipse season. Even SSO still needs batteries or throttling for months each year.
  • Downlink bandwidth, sky brightness for astronomers, and deorbiting km²-sized structures.
